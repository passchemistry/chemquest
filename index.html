      Filling rules

      Aufbau principle: fill lower-energy subshells before higher ones.

      Pauli exclusion principle: an orbital holds at most 2 electrons, and they have opposite spins.

      Hund’s rule: put one electron in each orbital of a subshell before pairing any up.

      Order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p 7s 5f 6d 7p. Subshells hold s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14. Energy level n holds 2n² electrons, so level 3 holds 18.

      Excited state: an electron absorbs energy and moves up, so the total stays the same (excited sodium: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 4s¹). Invalid: a subshell that does not exist (1p, 2d, 3f) or one holding too many electrons.

      The four quantum numbers

      n, principal: the energy level: 1, 2, 3 and up. Larger n means farther from the nucleus and higher energy.

      ℓ, angular momentum: the subshell and orbital shape. ℓ runs from 0 to n − 1: 0 = s, 1 = p, 2 = d, 3 = f.

      mℓ, magnetic: the orbital’s orientation, from −ℓ to +ℓ including 0.

      mₛ, spin: either +½ or −½.

      s is a sphere, p a dumbbell, d mostly a four-lobed cloverleaf. Orbitals per subshell: s 1, p 3, d 5, f 7. Each orbital holds 2 electrons.

      Level n has n² orbitals and holds 2n² electrons. Orbitals that do not exist: 1p, 2d, 3f, because ℓ must be less than n. If ℓ = 2, the smallest n is 3.

      A key that counts 32 electrons for n = 5 uses only s, p, d and f. The full rule gives 50, because n = 5 also has a g subshell.

      The electromagnetic spectrum

      Key relationships

      c = λν (c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s)E = hν (h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s)1 m = 10⁹ nm

      Wavelength and frequency are inversely related. Frequency and energy are directly related.

      Lowest to highest energy and frequency: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, UV, X-ray, gamma. Wavelength runs the other way.

      An electron absorbs energy and jumps up to an excited state. When it falls back, it releases the difference as a photon. Bigger drops give higher-frequency light.

      Every element has its own emission lines, so a spectrum identifies the elements in a star. A mixture shows the lines of every element in it. Lines shifted toward the red end mean the source is moving away from us.

      Key definitions

      Atomic number = protons. It sits above the symbol.

      Mass number = protons + neutrons. Always a whole number. From the table, round the atomic mass.

      Atomic mass = weighted average mass of all natural isotopes. Usually not a whole number.

      Neutrons = mass number − protons. Electrons = protons in a neutral atom.

      Ion: protons and electrons differ, so it has a charge. You can tell from one atom.

      Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. You need two or more atoms to compare.

      Mass spectrometer: band position shows mass, and a thicker band means a more abundant isotope.

      Trends at a glance

      Trends and why

      Atomic radius: decreases across (more protons pull electrons in), increases down (more shells).

      Ionization energy: increases across (smaller atom, harder to remove an electron), decreases down (easier). Highest at the top right.

      Electronegativity: increases across, decreases down. Fluorine is highest. Noble gases already have a full octet, so they do not attract more electrons.

      Shielding: inner electrons block part of the nucleus’s pull on the outer ones.

      Metals are large with weak pull, so they lose electrons. Nonmetals are small with strong pull, so they gain them. Francium is the most reactive alkali metal because it holds its outer electron least tightly.

      Families and facts

      Group 1 alkali metals, 2 alkaline earth, 3–12 transition metals, 13 boron group, 14 carbon group, 15 nitrogen group, 16 oxygen group, 17 halogens, 18 noble gases.

      Groups 1A–8A are the representative elements. Valence electrons: 1, 2, then 3 to 8 for groups 13 to 18. Columns share properties because they share valence electrons.

      Diatomic elements: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

      3-2-1 diagonal: Al is +3, Zn is +2, Ag is +1.

      Ionization energy order check: Mn, Cu, S, F runs lowest to highest.

      Accuracy, systems and mass

      Accuracy is how close a value is to the true value. Precision is how close repeated values are to each other.

      The system is what you are studying, such as steel wool. The surroundings are everything else, such as the fire and O₂. In a closed system, mass stays the same in a chemical reaction (conservation of mass).

      Laws of composition

      Definite proportions: a compound always has its elements in the same mass ratio. Water is about 1 g H to 8 g O in any sample.

      Multiple proportions: when two elements form different compounds, the masses of one that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in small whole-number ratios. H₂O and H₂O₂: for the same hydrogen, the oxygen is 1 : 2.

      Example: compound A has 42.0 g N per 105.1 g O, so 0.400 g N per g O. Compound B has 66.0 g N per 54.9 g O, so 1.20 g N per g O. The ratio 1.20 / 0.400 = 3 is a whole number.

      Classifying matter

      Element: pure, one kind of atom (Cl₂, Li). Compound: pure, different atoms chemically bonded (H₂O, CO₂). Mixture: unbonded substances, homogeneous (air, salt water) or heterogeneous (pizza, cereal).

      Separating mixtures needs only physical changes: filtration, distillation (boiling points), evaporation, magnets, and chromatography, where the parts move at different rates across the paper.

      Diatomic elements exist as pairs: H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂.

      Properties and changes

      Intensive properties do not depend on amount (color, density). Extensive ones do (mass, volume).

      Physical changes keep the same substance: melting, evaporating, crushing. Chemical changes make new substances: gas produced, rusting, light produced, reacting with sodium.

      Density = mass / volume. 500 g in 20 cm³ is 25 g/cm³, so 750 g takes up 750 / 25 = 30 cm³.

      Significant figures

      Counting: nonzero digits and zeros between them count. Leading zeros never count. Trailing zeros count only with a decimal point. 6,000 has 1, 1.0010 has 5, 0.00520 has 3.

      Multiply or divide: keep the fewest significant figures. Add or subtract: keep the fewest decimal places.

      Reading instruments: read every marked digit and estimate exactly one more.
